One spacecraft, one controller, four ways to make the torque: reaction wheels, control moment gyroscopes, thrusters, and wheels whose momentum is dumped by magnetic torque rods. All four turn to the same target and then hold it against the same small, steady disturbance, so each one's strengths and limits show side by side.

About

The spacecraft (J = diag(100, 120, 80) kg·m²) starts at rest and turns by θ, then holds. A small steady torque fixed in space, such as sunlight pressure on a lopsided craft, pushes on it the whole time.

Control moment gyros

Four single-gimbal CMGs in a pyramid. Each rotor spins at constant speed; turning its gimbal swings its momentum, and the reaction is the torque.

Thrusters

Pairs fire together as couples, 1.2 m apart, so they twist without pushing. Each pulse burns propellant (Isp = 220 s).

Magnetic torque rods

Three rods make a magnet m; Earth's field B twists it with g = m × B, never along B. Circular 500 km orbit, 95 min period, at inclination i.

What is happening

—

    New to this? Key terms
    Actuator
    Anything that produces a torque to turn the spacecraft or hold it still.
    Reaction wheel
    A flywheel driven by a motor. Spinning it up one way turns the spacecraft the other way, so momentum moves between wheel and body without leaving the spacecraft.
    Saturation
    A wheel has a top speed, so it can store only so much momentum. A steady outside push keeps adding momentum until the wheels are full; after that they cannot hold the spacecraft.
    Momentum dumping (desaturation)
    Using an outside torque (thrusters or torque rods) to remove stored momentum, so the wheels can slow down while the spacecraft holds still.
    Control moment gyroscope (CMG)
    A rotor spinning at constant high speed in a motorized gimbal. Tilting the gimbal swings the rotor's large momentum, and the reaction is a large torque. Some gimbal combinations cannot give torque in a certain direction (a singularity).
    Thruster couple
    Two thrusters firing in opposite directions on opposite sides, so they twist the spacecraft without pushing it along.
    Deadband and limit cycle
    Thrusters are either on or off, so they fire only when the error leaves a small band. The spacecraft then drifts back and forth across the band forever.
    Magnetic torque rod
    An electromagnet. Earth's magnetic field twists it, like a compass needle. It needs no propellant, but is weak and can never twist about the field direction.
    Specific impulse Isp
    How efficiently a thruster uses propellant: the impulse it gives per weight of propellant burned, in seconds.

      Model

      Body, with internal momentum (Lesson 26)
      Jω = gext − ω × (Jω + h) − h
      Controller (the same for every lane)
      ωref = −(kp/kd) e, capped at ωmax;  gcmd = kdJ(ωref − ω) + ω × (Jω + h)
      Actuators
      wheels: h = −gcmd, split by a pseudo-inverse, |τi| ≤ τmax, |hi| ≤ hmax
      CMGs: h = h0Σ δi(gi × ĥi), steered with a singularity-robust inverse
      thrusters: ±FL per axis when |gcmd| exceeds the deadband
      torque rods: m = B × gcmd/|B|²,  g = m × B ⟂ B

      All four lanes integrate the full nonlinear equations and the attitude quaternion with fixed-step RK4, holding each actuator's output constant over the step (0.1 s; at most 0.05 s, and a whole fraction of the minimum on-time, for the thrusters; 0.5 s for rods alone). The controller is a rate-limited quaternion PD (natural frequency 0.15 rad/s, rate limit 2°/s). Rods alone use a much gentler version (0.0007 rad/s, 0.05°/s): magnetic control is only stable when it is slow compared with the orbit. The field is a tilted dipole; dumping uses m = k(h × B)/|B|², which removes the part of the wheel momentum across B.
