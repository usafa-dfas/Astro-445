Gimbal Lock Explorer

Three nested gimbals whose angles are exactly a set of Euler angles. When the middle gimbal reaches its singular value, the outer and inner axes line up, the three axes span only a plane, and the payload can no longer turn in one direction. The payload's motion stays smooth; the angles that describe it do not.

The lost direction n̂

n̂ in A
n̂ in B
φ axis · n̂ = det S

n̂ is perpendicular to the outer two gimbal axes, which are always perpendicular to each other. The inner axis has a component of det S along n̂. At lock that is zero: no combination of gimbal rates turns the payload about n̂.

SequenceθMargin

Every sequence has a singularity somewhere, but no attitude is singular in all of them. Gimbal lock belongs to the description, not to the attitude.

How it is avoided

Flight software carries the attitude as a quaternion, which has no singularity, and converts to Euler angles only for display (Lesson 4, App. A). Hardware gimbal platforms either add a fourth gimbal or keep the vehicle's maneuvers away from the lock attitude. The Apollo guidance platform had three gimbals and warned the crew as the middle gimbal angle approached lock.

The commanded attitude is integrated as a quaternion and never misbehaves. The gimbal angles are integrated from ȧ = S−1(ω + 2e), where e is the small rotation from the gimbal attitude to the commanded one, using a damped inverse so that exact lock gives a finite answer. Steps shrink so that no gimbal turns more than 0.5° per step. The dashed angles in the strip are taken from the commanded DCM with θ in its standard range, as a MATLAB script would.
