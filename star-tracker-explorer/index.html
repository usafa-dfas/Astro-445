Star Tracker Explorer

A star tracker photographs the sky, recognizes the stars, and knows where each one is in an inertial catalog. Each star gives a direction measured in the body frame, bi, and the same direction from the catalog, ri. Finding the attitude that best rotates every ri onto its bi is Wahba's problem, solved here with Davenport's q-method.

Star tracker

The tracker looks along the body axis b̂3 (its boresight). It reports the brightest stars inside its field, already identified against the catalog.

Sun sensor

The notes weight every vector equally. A sun sensor is thousands of times less precise than a star, so equal weights let it drag the answer off. Wahba's original problem has a weight on each vector for exactly this.

Appendix A sensors

The directions to the Sun and of the magnetic field are known in the inertial frame from an ephemeris and a field model. A tilt error moves a reading out of place by that angle, as in Step 4 of the appendix.

Gyro propagation

Between fixes the attitude is found by integrating the gyro's rates with the kinematic equation, so any bias builds up as drift. A star fix resets it. Real flight software blends the two with a Kalman filter and estimates the bias too; this is the simplest version of the idea.

    New to this? Key terms
    Attitude determination
    Working out which way the spacecraft is turned, from what its sensors measure.
    Reference vector r and body vector b
    The same direction (to a star, the Sun, along the magnetic field) written two ways: r in the inertial frame, from a catalog or model, and b in the body frame, as the sensor measures it. If the attitude were known exactly, b = CB/Ar.
    Wahba's problem
    Find the rotation that brings every r as close as possible to its b. With perfect sensors it fits exactly; with real ones it is the best compromise.
    Davenport's q-method
    Packs all the measurements into one 4 × 4 matrix K. The best quaternion is the eigenvector of K with the largest eigenvalue.
    Eigenvalue and eigenvector
    Special directions q that K only stretches: Kq = λq. A 4 × 4 matrix has four. Here each is a candidate attitude, and its λ says how well it fits.
    Residual n − λmax
    Zero when the measurements agree perfectly; it grows with their disagreement. A free quality check.
    Boresight, pointing and roll
    The boresight is the direction the camera looks. Pointing error tips the boresight; roll error spins the picture around it. A narrow camera sees roll much less well than pointing.
    Field of view and limiting magnitude
    How much sky the camera sees, and how faint a star it can detect. Bigger magnitudes are fainter stars.
    Gyro bias and drift
    A gyro measures rotation rate, and adding up those rates tracks the attitude. A small constant error in the rate (bias) adds up to an ever-growing attitude error (drift).
    Kalman filter
    The method real spacecraft use to blend gyro rates and vector measurements, weighting each by how much it can be trusted. It is beyond this course.

      Quaternions are scalar first, q = (η, ε), with CB/A = (η² − εTε)I + 2εεT − 2η[ε×] as in Lesson 4. Frame A is the J2000 equatorial frame: â1 toward the vernal equinox, â3 toward the north celestial pole. The truth turns at a constant body rate; the gyro estimate is integrated with fixed-step RK4. Every measured and catalog vector is a unit vector before it enters B. The eigenvalues come from a Jacobi sweep, which is exact for a symmetric 4 × 4 matrix, and the largest is picked explicitly. Stars are assumed already identified; real trackers match star patterns against the catalog first.
